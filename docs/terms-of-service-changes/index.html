Terms of service changes in 2026: dated tracker

16 changes logged · Updated October 21, 2026

Terms of service updates decide what you can dispute, what a company can do with your money, and how disagreements get settled. This page lists every terms change our daily check has caught, newest first, in plain English with a link to the source text.

  • Terms Shopify

    Tax tools now fall under one Shopify Tax terms document

    Shopify's Terms of Service used to say that tools that help you calculate or estimate taxes were covered by the Shopify Tax Terms of Service, and that tools that help you file taxes ("Shopify Tax Automated Filing") were covered by separate Automated Filing terms. The section now says access to "any tax-related tools that Shopify offers to you" is subject to the Shopify Tax Terms of Service, and the Automated Filing reference is gone.

  • Terms Proton

    Proton terms: deletion warnings for inactive free accounts are now 'a mere courtesy' (Oct 2026)

    Proton edited the inactive-accounts section of its Terms of Service, and the page still says "Last modified: October 09, 2026." Free accounts inactive for 12 months or more can have the account and some or all of its data deleted, including Proton Mail emails, Proton Drive files, Proton Calendar entries and Proton Pass passwords. Until yesterday the terms promised: "You will receive notices 30, 15 and 7 days in advance before such action is taken on the recovery email associated with your Account." As of October 20, 2026 that sentence reads: "As a mere courtesy, the Company will aim to send notice to the recovery email associated with your Account 30, 15 and 7 days in advance before such action is taken." The warnings went from a promise to a best effort. Paid accounts are still always treated as active, and logging in on the web, desktop or mobile apps at least once every 12 months still counts as activity.

  • Terms Pinterest

    Pinterest terms change Nov 12, 2026: one arbitrator for mass claims, TV ads by household, 30-day arbitration opt-out

    Pinterest is replacing its Terms of Service and Privacy Policy on November 12, 2026. Both new versions have been posted as previews since about September 1. Pinterest's help page says "Your Pinterest experience won't change" and "By continuing to use Pinterest after November 12, 2026, you accept the updates to the Terms." The biggest change is to Section 11, the arbitration agreement that covers everyone outside the EEA, Switzerland and the UK, which has been rewritten from scratch. Arbitration moves from the American Arbitration Association to JAMS. The new agreement "applies to any Claim you have not filed in court or in arbitration before the date you agreed to these Terms, and supersedes and amends any prior arbitration agreement between you and Pinterest, including as to Claims that arose before this or any prior arbitration agreement." Each case is "a single, confidential arbitration." Mass claims are squeezed harder. Today, if 25 or more similar demands are filed within 60 days or with the same law firms, AAA groups them into batches and appoints "one arbitrator to resolve each batch." Under the new text, when 25 or more claimants file similar claims within 90 days or through coordinated lawyers, "all of those Claims will be consolidated into one arbitration before one arbitrator." If a lawyer or litigation funder is advancing a claimant's costs, the arbitrator "may also divide all fees and costs (including all arbitrator's fees) between that funder and Pinterest." In the final decision, the arbitrator can divide fees "among Pinterest, the claimants, and the claimants' lawyers." Either side can also make a written settlement offer under California Code of Civil Procedure section 998. Under that rule, a party who turns down an offer and then does worse can be made to pay the other side's costs from the date of the offer. Requests for public injunctive relief go to a court. One change works in users' favor: the terms add a 30-day opt-out. "You may opt out of this Section 11 within 30 days of first becoming subject to it by emailing litigation@pinterest.com with your name, account email, and a statement that you're opting out." The current terms (effective April 30, 2025) have no opt-out. The new terms also say that, for users outside the EEA, Switzerland and the UK, "your acceptance of these Terms constitutes your consent to the processing activities described in our Privacy Policy under the laws of your jurisdiction," and that "By accessing YouTube content on Pinterest, you also agree to be bound by YouTube's Terms of Service." The new Privacy Policy, also effective November 12, describes ads that follow you off Pinterest. Its example: if you look at camping gear, "we may show you ads for other outdoor products on Pinterest, or, for example, depending on your location, when you're streaming a show on your connected TV." Ad targeting now includes "associating devices that may relate to the same person or household." Pinterest says it may get information "from partners that help us power the ads we show to you on and off Pinterest" and, "Depending on where you live," "from data providers." "Voice transcripts" join the activity Pinterest uses "to make inferences about you and your preferences." A new line says Pinterest "may also use, or work with vendors who use automated tools to scan and analyze your messages to detect harmful or unlawful content." According to PPC Land, Pinterest bought the connected-TV ad company tvScientific in February 2026. Coverage so far (checked October 20): PPC Land covered the advertiser-side changes (ad guidelines and connected-TV ad contracts) on September 12. We found no reporting on the user arbitration rewrite, the new opt-out, or the household and TV ad language. What you can do: to keep your right to go to court, email litigation@pinterest.com with your name, your account email and a statement that you're opting out of Section 11. For existing users the new terms take effect November 12, so the safest window is November 12 to December 12, 2026. Keep a copy of your email. If you use Global Privacy Control in a U.S. state that honors it, the new policy says to "log into your Pinterest account from a web browser with the Global Privacy Control enabled to link that opt-out choice to your account." It also says "Your connected TV may allow you to manage or limit the use of data for advertising." For comparison, Discord's terms also let users decline arbitration by email ("emailing an opt-out notice to arbitration-opt-out@discord.com within 30 days" of its September 29, 2025 terms or of registering), and Discord's mass-arbitration section lets either side opt out to court if a batch can't be resolved. Pinterest's new version goes the other way: 25 or more similar claims end up in one arbitration before one arbitrator. A plain opt-out email (our wording, not Pinterest's): To: litigation@pinterest.com. Subject: Opt-out of Section 11 arbitration. Body: "My name is [full name] and my Pinterest account email is [email]. I am opting out of Section 11 (Arbitration) of the Pinterest Terms of Service effective November 12, 2026." Send it on or after November 12 and before December 12, 2026, and save the sent copy.

  • Terms Google Play

    Google Play Terms of Service update Nov 17, 2026: what changes (defective content, Play services, trials)

    Google is replacing the Google Play Terms of Service on November 17, 2026. The current terms page, dated July 29, 2026, now says: "We're updating our Play Terms of Service on November 17, 2026. Until then, the Terms below continue to apply." It links to a preview dated November 17, 2026. According to Open Terms Archive's captures, the notice first appeared around October 5 with a November 5 date and was changed to November 17 on October 20. We compared the preview with the current terms line by line. The edits are small, and most of them shift responsibility away from Google. Defective purchases: today the terms tell you to check what you bought and "notify us or the Provider as soon as reasonably possible if you find any errors or defect." The new version says to "notify the Provider," so Google drops itself as a contact. In the cancellation clause, the line that you won't get a refund for the current billing period "except as otherwise provided in Google Play's Refund Policy (for example where Content is defective)" loses its "(for example where Content is defective)" example. Scope: the "Content" the Play terms cover now includes "system services (including, but not limited to, those provided by Google Play services)," in addition to the apps, games, books and other content "available through the Play Store." So the rules for Content now apply to Google's background system software too. The July 29 update already made you responsible for carrier data charges from system services, including in the background when the screen is locked. Free trials: the trial clause now covers access "at no charge for a specified trial period" and drops the words "or at a reduced price," so discounted intro offers are no longer described as trials there. Taxes can now be charged if "the Provider or Google or its affiliates" must collect them (it used to say "the seller of Content or Google"). "Google may allow you to download, view or use Content free of charge" becomes "You may be permitted to." Unchanged: at least 30 days' notice of terms changes, at least 30 days' notice before a subscription price increase applies, and charges up to 48 hours before each billing period. Coverage (checked October 20): ConductAtlas logged the November 5 notice on October 6, and Android Authority covered the July 29 update. We found no reporting on what the November 17 version changes. What you can do: if something you bought on Google Play doesn't work, report it to the developer and also request a refund through Google Play's refund process, keeping records of both. Under the new wording, the developer is the party you're told to notify.

  • Terms USPS

    USPS rule (Oct 19, 2026): senders billed for unpaid foreign duties on packages, even if disputed, 6% interest

    The U.S. Postal Service published an interim final rule in the Federal Register on October 19, 2026 (FR Doc. 2026-21310, "Customs Requirements and Payment Procedures for Certain Customs Charges") that took effect the same day. It rewrites the International Mail Manual so the person who mails a package abroad can be billed later for the destination country's duties, taxes and import fees. A new section 424, "Unpaid Destination Customs Charges," says that if a customs authority, postal operator or broker in the destination country "seeks to collect duties, taxes, or import fees from the mailer or the Postal Service" and they "have not been paid in full, the Postal Service (or its representative) will send an invoice to the mailer for such amounts." The mailer "must pay the Postal Service ... the entire amount due in accordance with the invoice terms, even if the mailer disputes the assessment," and is not relieved of the charge "regardless of whether the item is delivered, lost, damaged, or returned." Late payment accrues "interest ... at a rate of 6 percent per annum," and USPS can recover its collection costs and may treat the unpaid amount as a postage revenue deficiency. You can dispute only USPS accounting errors. Any challenge to the foreign charge itself "must be addressed directly to such party without involvement from the Postal Service," and USPS says it "is not responsible for the accuracy" of those charges. The rule also says that where a destination country requires a delivered duty paid (DDP) shipment, "the mailer must send the mailpiece on a delivered duty paid basis," and that duties and fees "are not included in the postage paid by the mailer." USPS says the trigger is the European Union ending its de minimis exemption for most low-value goods on July 1, 2026. Under the new EU rules, some EU postal operators require prepaid duties, charge fees when customs data is wrong, or go after the sender when the recipient doesn't pay. USPS's own prepaid-duties page says EU countries now charge a flat, nonrefundable €3 duty on merchandise up to €150 and on gifts worth over €45 up to €150, and that USPS Delivered Duty Paid is built into retail counters and Click-N-Ship. The rule text itself isn't limited to the EU: it applies wherever a destination party seeks unpaid charges. USPS is taking comments until November 18, 2026. Coverage so far (checked October 19): a legal-news summary (LexVira), a personal-finance blog (Main Street Dollars) and a small online outlet (The Digital Free Press). We found no major news outlet reporting it. What it replaces (checked October 19, 2026): the International Mail Manual still posted on USPS's Postal Explorer site hasn't been updated yet. Its section 131.34 says "No provision is made for prepayment of customs duty on mail addressed for delivery in foreign countries," that "most countries will collect from the recipient," and that duties and fees "are not included in the postage paid by the mailer." It has no section 424 and doesn't mention invoicing the sender. How private carriers compare: FedEx's duties-and-taxes page says that if the recipient refuses the package or refuses to pay, "the original shipper will be billed for duties and taxes." UPS tells shippers "if the receiver does not pay, UPS may recover the outstanding amounts from you." DHL Express's Terms and Conditions of Carriage say the shipper "shall pay or reimburse DHL for all Customs Duties and other charges ... if Consignee has failed to pay." So USPS is moving toward how commercial carriers already work. Its version goes further in writing: pay "even if the mailer disputes the assessment," keep owing it if the item is lost or returned, 6% interest, and disputes limited to USPS accounting errors. What you can do before you mail: prepay with USPS Delivered Duty Paid. It's available only at the time of mailing, on Priority Mail Express International, Priority Mail International and First-Class Package International Service, through a retail counter, Click-N-Ship, USPS APIs or Global Shipping Software, and only to select countries. USPS's July 1, 2026 Postal Bulletin added 21 destinations, mostly EU countries and territories, including France, Italy, Spain and the Netherlands. Describe each item accurately on the customs form, because USPS uses the descriptions to assign HS codes and charges the €3 duty "for each unique HS Code." The European Commission says the €3 applies per item type (one T-shirt plus one watch is €6), that the seller or importer is normally the one who pays, and that product identifiers become mandatory on November 1, 2026. Etsy's help center recommends DDP shipping to reduce "refused shipments, and unexpected charges at delivery." eBay sellers using eBay International Shipping send items to a U.S. hub instead, and eBay says import fees are collected from the buyer at checkout or on delivery. How to comment: this is an interim final rule, so it's already in force, but USPS is taking comments until November 18, 2026. The Federal Register notice says to email PCFederalRegister@usps.gov with the subject line "Customs Requirements and Payment Procedures" and your name and address, or mail comments to the Manager of Product Classification, U.S. Postal Service, 475 L'Enfant Plaza SW, Room 4446, Washington, DC 20260-5015. Faxed comments aren't accepted, and on October 19 regulations.gov listed the document (USPS-2026-2511-0001) as not open for comment there.

  • Terms Oura

    Oura terms update (Nov 4, 2026): accept in the app or lose access; jury waiver and mass-arbitration batches added

    Oura's Terms of Use now read "Last Updated: October 2, 2026" and "Effective Date: November 4, 2026," replacing the April 20, 2026 version. An Oura help-center article (last updated October 13) says members must accept the new terms in the Oura App by November 4: "After that date, you can't use the Oura App until you accept." The biggest rewrite is the U.S. dispute section. The old terms had one paragraph requiring arbitration and waiving class actions. The new Section XVI has 12 numbered parts. Disputes go to the American Arbitration Association (AAA), the provision "includes a class action and jury trial waiver," it covers any dispute "regardless of when it accrued," and it "supersedes all prior versions." Before filing, you must mail a written "Pre-Dispute Notice" to Oura's agent for service of process in Sacramento and negotiate for 30 days. Claims of $50,000 or less are decided on written submissions unless the arbitrator decides to hold a hearing. A new "Mass Arbitration Procedures" section applies when 25 or more similar claims are filed by people whose lawyers work together: 20 randomly picked cases go first, the rest are paused (with deadlines tolled) until those are decided and a mediation is held, then the next batch of 20 proceeds. Claims exempt from arbitration include small-claims cases, public injunctive relief under California law, and "any claim or cause of action for vexatious litigation." Neither version offers an arbitration opt-out. Other changes: the old promise to email you "at least thirty (30) days in advance" of a fee change is replaced by notice "in advance of the effective date and within the timeframe required by applicable law," plus a new right to cancel before a new fee applies. The liability cap changes from $100 to "the amount paid to Oura in the prior twelve (12) months." The old one-year deadline for filing claims is gone. Renewal charges move from one day before your billing anniversary to the anniversary itself, and you must cancel at least one day before renewal. The data section now says Oura "disclaims all liability that arises out of or in any way relates to any information that you provide or direct to be provided to Oura, even if that information constitutes electronic patient health records," and that Oura "may use, process, or share it, consistent with" the terms and its Privacy Policy. Oura says the update "does not change how Oura collects, uses, or protects member data," and its Privacy Policy is still dated June 15, 2026. The terms also add a "Regulated Features" section that names the Fertile Window feature in Cycle Insights as a medical device in the EU. How it compares (checked October 17, 2026): WHOOP's Terms of Use (last updated August 3, 2026) also require arbitration with a class action and jury trial waiver, but say "UNLESS YOU OPT OUT OF THE AGREEMENT TO ARBITRATE WITHIN 30 DAYS," and let members reject an updated agreement by emailing support@whoop.com within 30 days, keeping the prior terms until their current membership ends. Eight Sleep's Terms of Service (last updated September 16, 2026) say "YOU MAY OPT OUT OF ARBITRATION WITHIN 30 DAYS OF FIRST AGREEING TO THESE TERMS" by writing to legal@eightsleep.com. Oura's new terms offer no arbitration opt-out and no way to reject the update except to stop using the app ("your only recourse is to cease using the Services").

  • Terms Robinhood

    Robinhood Class Action Service: auto-enrollment on Nov 18, 2026 and a 20% fee (how to opt out)

  • Terms U.S. DOT

    DOT delays airline "controllable delay" change to Feb 1, 2027: maintenance, medical, unruly-passenger delays

    A Federal Register notice published October 15, 2026 (FR Doc 2026-21058) delays the effective date of DOT's September 3, 2026 final rule on airline delay and cancellation categories under Section 511(b) of the FAA Reauthorization Act. The rule, which creates a new category for ten events that will no longer count as "Air Carrier" (controllable) delays or cancellations—including certain unscheduled maintenance, removing an unruly passenger, medical emergencies, cyberattacks, and government system failures—now takes effect February 1, 2027 instead of October 19, 2026. The new categories apply to flights on or after January 1, 2027, so holiday-season flights stay under the old categories. DOT's earlier analysis said the change is expected to reduce the number of delays and cancellations for which carriers provide amenities and compensation under voluntary commitments.
