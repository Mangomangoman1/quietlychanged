How to stop robotexts and robocalls: the FCC's reply-STOP rule from Nov 25, 2026

Short answer: reply with exactly the word the text names (STOP is safest), or use the key-press option on a robocall. From November 25, 2026, a company can make that its only accepted way to opt out, and a STOP to one type of alert may not stop the others.

Published October 30, 2026

What changes on November 25, 2026

The Federal Communications Commission adopted a new order on robocall and robotext opt-outs (FCC 26-67) on September 30, 2026. It was published in the Federal Register on October 26, 2026 with the line "Effective November 25, 2026." It covers the automated calls and texts that need your consent: autodialed or prerecorded calls and automated texts to your phone, such as payment reminders, delivery and appointment alerts, bank notices and marketing texts. Ordinary calls from a person aren't covered.

Two things change for you:

  • A company can pick the only way you're allowed to opt out. The new rule says "Callers may designate any of the following methods as the exclusive means to revoke consent": an automated voice or key-press opt-out on the call, a reply to a text with one of seven words, or a website or phone number the company picks. A company that does this "is not required to process revocation requests made by any other means."
  • Stopping one kind of alert may not stop the rest. For informational messages (reminders, alerts, account notices), a company may treat your opt-out "as applying only to the specific category of informational robocalls and text messages to which the revocation was directed and not to all communications."

Before vs after: what counts as opting out

Until November 25 (the rule in effect since April 2025), you can revoke consent "by using any reasonable method to clearly express a desire not to receive further calls or text messages." A reply in your own words must count "if a reasonable person would understand those words to have conveyed a request to revoke consent," a voicemail or email to the company creates a presumption that you opted out, and callers "may not designate an exclusive means to request revocation of consent."

From November 25, if a company has named an exclusive method, only that method has to be honored. A text like "please take me off your list," an email to customer service, or telling a phone agent may no longer legally count. Companies that don't name an exclusive method must keep accepting any reasonable request.

The seven reply words that always count for texts are STOP, QUIT, END, REVOKE, OPT OUT, CANCEL and UNSUBSCRIBE. A company has to disclose its chosen method "clearly and conspicuously" on the call or in each text. A text only needs to mention one of the seven words, but the sender must still honor all seven.

How to stop robotexts and robocalls from November 25

  1. Read the opt-out line at the bottom of the text and use exactly that. Reply with the word it names (STOP is the safest), or use the number or link it gives.
  2. On a robocall, use the key-press option if one is offered.
  3. Opt out of each type of message separately. If one company sends you payment reminders, appointment alerts and delivery texts, reply STOP to each type you want gone. Or ask the company to stop everything through its named method, and keep a screenshot.
  4. Write down the date. Companies must honor an opt-out "within a reasonable time not to exceed ten business days." If messages keep coming after that, your record shows when you asked.
  5. No reply possible? Senders whose texting systems can't receive replies must say so in each text and offer another way to opt out. Use that way.

Marketing texts are different

A STOP to a telemarketing or advertising message still revokes consent to all future marketing robocalls and texts from that caller. The category-by-category rule applies to informational messages such as payment reminders, delivery alerts and appointment reminders. The FCC's own example is a consumer who stops payment reminders but still wants fraud alerts.

Bank fraud alerts

Under the FCC's exemption for fraud and security alerts, banks could send these to a cell number only if it was "provided by the customer." From November 25 they can also use a wireless number "obtained from a reliable source": one supplied by a spouse or family member authorized on the account, captured when you call the bank, or in records from another financial institution. You can still opt out of those messages, and the exemption's limits on how many calls and texts can be sent still apply.

The "revoke all" rule that never took effect

A 2024 FCC rule that would have made one opt-out cover everything from a sender never took effect; it was put on hold by waiver, most recently until January 31, 2027, according to Nixon Peabody. The new order replaces it.

Want a one-step "stop everything"? Comment by November 25

In a companion proposal (FR Doc. 2026-21820, also published October 26), the FCC asks whether to shorten the ten-business-day deadline (it floats seven), require two-way texting, require a one-step "revoke all" option, and how opt-outs should apply to a company's affiliates. Comments are due November 25, 2026, and replies December 28, 2026, in CG Docket No. 02-278.

  1. Open the FCC's ECFS Express form.
  2. Enter 02-278 under Proceeding(s) and pick CG Docket No. 02-278.
  3. Add your name and mailing address, type your view in Brief Comments, and submit by midnight Eastern on November 25. Comments filed with the FCC are made public.

The FCC's guide How to comment on FCC proceedings covers anything not listed here. For the exact rule text, read the Federal Register notice.
