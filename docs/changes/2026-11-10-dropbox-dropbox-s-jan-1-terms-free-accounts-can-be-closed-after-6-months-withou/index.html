Dropbox terms change Jan 1, 2027: free accounts closed after 6 months inactive, 18+ only

From Jan 1, 2027, Dropbox can close a free account after 6 months without use, down from 12. Users must be 18, and keeping an account means you accept the new terms.

Takes effect Jan 1, 2027in 51 days

What changed

Dropbox posted new Terms of Service and a new Privacy Policy on September 1, 2026. Both take effect January 1, 2027. We compared the new terms (dropbox.com/terms2026, marked "Posted: September 1, 2026" and "Effective: January 1, 2027") with the terms in force today ("Posted: January 7, 2025"), and did the same for the privacy policy (in force since May 30, 2025). We fetched all four on Nov 10, 2026. Dropbox's announcement leads with a new feature, using one account for several personal and team workspaces. Several changes for free users are listed further down.

Change 1: Free accounts can be closed after 6 months, not 12

The current terms let Dropbox suspend or terminate your access with notice if you "don't have a Paid Account and haven't accessed our Services for 12 consecutive months." The new terms say "6 consecutive months." The rest of the clause stays the same: Dropbox says it will give "reasonable advance notice via the email address associated with your account" and a chance to "export Your Stuff" first. Dropbox's announcement adds: "We'll continue to provide notice before doing so."

This matters most to people on Dropbox Basic, the free 2 GB plan, who keep old files there and rarely sign in. Dropbox's help page on inactive accounts (checked Nov 10, 2026) still describes the old rule. It says activity means "log-ins and file activity (adding, editing, or deleting) anywhere on Dropbox during the past 12 months," and that "Logging in again will reset the 12-month clock." The page also says: "If we don't notify you that your account is inactive, your account won't be deleted due to inactivity," and that you'll get "multiple reminder emails" with the scheduled deletion date. Activity on Dropbox Sign or DocSend "doesn't count."

When the clock matters: if the six-month test applies as soon as the terms take effect, a free account nobody has signed in to since before July 1, 2026 would already be past six months on January 1, 2027. Dropbox hasn't said whether it counts inactivity from before January 1.

Change 2: You must be 18 to use Dropbox

The current terms say you must be "at least 13 if you reside in the United States, and 16 if you reside anywhere else." The new terms say "you must be at least 18," and add: "Dropbox may use and rely on information from third parties, including age signals from app stores, for the purpose of enforcing this restriction." Dropbox's help page says: "Starting January 1, 2027, you must be at least 18 years old in order to use Dropbox." Neither the terms nor the help pages say what happens to accounts that 13- to 17-year-olds already have, or to their files.

Change 3: To say no, you now have to delete your account

The current terms say that if you don't agree to an update, "please cancel your account and stop using the Services," and that "continuing to use or access the Services" means you agree. The new terms say "please cancel and delete your account," and "By maintaining an account after the updates come into effect, you agree to be bound by the revised Terms." Dropbox's help page spells it out: "You will agree to our updated Terms by maintaining a Dropbox account after the effective date of the updated Terms." So an account you never open still counts as agreeing on January 1.

Other changes

Refunds: "Refunds are only issued if required by law" becomes "Refunds are only issued in limited circumstances or if required by law." Dropbox's help page calls this a clarification that "we may also grant refunds in other limited circumstances." The terms don't say what those circumstances are.

Price-change notice: the promise of "no less than 30 days' advance notice" of a new renewal price now starts with "Unless a shorter notice period is necessitated by applicable law." You still get the chance to cancel before the new fee.

Several accounts on one email: the new terms treat each team or personal workspace you join as "its own account" and say "Actions taken on one account, including suspension, deletion, termination, or removal from a Dropbox Team, may impact your access to or use of the Services." If an admin removes you from a team and deletes that account, "you may be unable to access your other accounts until you change the email address for those accounts." And: "If you have multiple accounts associated with the same email address, and we suspend or terminate your access to the Services, that action may apply to all associated accounts."

Work email addresses: today, your organization "may be able to block" a personal Dropbox account opened with a work address. The new terms add that "even if your organization does not block your use of Dropbox, we may block your use of Dropbox" until you move to a Dropbox Team or switch to a personal email address.

Privacy policy: the new policy adds that team admins "may be able to view changes in account state for your other accounts, such as suspension, deletion, termination, account capture, or removal from another Dropbox Team," and a way to appeal privacy decisions (email privacy@dropbox.com). It also now points straight to its FAQ "for more detail regarding how Your Stuff may be used to improve the Services." That FAQ says (the sentence has been there since at least April 2025, per the Wayback Machine): "We may build models that identify keywords and topics from a given document. These models may be trained on your documents and metadata, and power features within Dropbox such as improved search relevance, auto-sorting and organization features, and document summaries."

How 6 months compares

Google says it may delete a personal Google Account "if you are inactive across Google for at least two years" (Google Account Help, checked Nov 10, 2026). Microsoft's Services Agreement (effective Sept 30, 2026) says you "must sign into your Outlook.com inbox and your OneDrive (separately) at least once in a one-year period, otherwise we will close your Outlook.com inbox and your OneDrive for you," and sign in to the Microsoft account itself at least once every two years. From January 1, Dropbox's limit for free accounts is half of OneDrive's and a quarter of Google's. Among other cloud storage services (terms checked Nov 10, 2026): Apple's iCloud terms say Apple may terminate an account "upon 30 days’ prior notice via email" if it "has been inactive for one (1) year"; pCloud says "Free pCloud accounts that have been inactive for 12 months, will be permanently deleted, along with the files inside"; MEGA is stricter than Dropbox, listing as a reason to suspend or terminate that "you are using a free account and that account has been inactive for over 3 months and you have been given notice to act."

In order, from longest to shortest: Google 2 years; Microsoft (Outlook.com and OneDrive) 1 year; Apple iCloud 1 year; pCloud 12 months; Dropbox 12 months today, 6 months from January 1, 2027; MEGA over 3 months, after notice. Proton went the other way this month: it now waits 24 months, not 12, before it can delete an unused free account (see our Nov 6 report on Proton).

What you can do: If you have a free Dropbox account you care about, sign in at dropbox.com or in the app before January 1 and at least every few months after that. Dropbox counts log-ins and adding, editing or deleting files. Watch for Dropbox emails about inactivity, including at old email addresses: the help page notes that many people have more than one account. If you'd rather not accept the new terms, the only way out is to download your files and delete your account before January 1, 2027. Under-18s, and parents of teens with accounts, should download any files they want to keep now.

Coverage (checked Nov 10, 2026): Dropbox announced the update on its blog on Sept 1, 2026, and in its help center. SMBtech (Sept 20) covered the 18+ age limit and the six-month window; Security Online, WebProNews and the UK site TrustList covered the six-month window; and the independent Dropbox Watchdog site published a clause-by-clause comparison that also noted the inactive-account help page still says 12 months. We found no coverage in major consumer or tech outlets (rechecked Nov 10, 2026), and none that compared the new limit with Google, Microsoft, Apple iCloud, pCloud and MEGA.

Who it affects

Anyone with a free Dropbox Basic account, especially people who keep old files there and rarely sign in, plus users aged 13 to 17 and anyone with several Dropbox accounts on one email address.

Quick answers

When do Dropbox's new terms take effect?

January 1, 2027. Dropbox posted the new Terms of Service and Privacy Policy on September 1, 2026, at dropbox.com/terms2026 and dropbox.com/privacy2026.

Will Dropbox delete my free account if I don't use it?

It can. From January 1, 2027, the terms let Dropbox suspend or terminate access to a free account that hasn't accessed the service for 6 consecutive months, down from 12. Dropbox says it will email you first and give you a chance to export your files. Its help page says it sends multiple reminders with the scheduled deletion date, and that an account won't be deleted for inactivity if you weren't notified.

What counts as activity on Dropbox?

Dropbox's help page says it looks at log-ins and file activity (adding, editing, or deleting) anywhere on Dropbox. Activity on Dropbox Sign or DocSend doesn't count. Signing in to dropbox.com, the desktop app or the mobile app resets the clock.

Does this affect paid Dropbox plans?

The inactivity rule only applies to accounts without a Paid Account. The 18+ age limit, the new refund wording, the multi-account rules and the 'maintaining an account' acceptance apply to all personal accounts. Dropbox team customers are covered by the separate Dropbox Services Agreement, which also now requires users to be over 18.

I'm under 18. Can I still use Dropbox?

Not after January 1, 2027. The new terms say you must be at least 18 and that Dropbox may use app-store age signals to enforce it. Dropbox hasn't said what happens to existing teen accounts, so download any files you want to keep.

How do I reject Dropbox's new terms?

You can't keep the account and reject them. Dropbox's help page says you agree 'by maintaining a Dropbox account after the effective date', and that if you don't agree you must cancel any subscription and delete your account before January 1, 2027.

Does Dropbox use my files to train AI?

Dropbox's privacy FAQ, which the new policy now points to, says it may build models that identify keywords and topics in documents and that 'these models may be trained on your documents and metadata' to power search, auto-sorting and document summaries. That FAQ wording has been there since at least April 2025 (Wayback Machine); it isn't new on January 1.

How does Dropbox's 6-month limit compare with Google and OneDrive?

Google may delete a personal Google Account after at least two years of inactivity. Microsoft closes an Outlook.com inbox and OneDrive if you don't sign in to each at least once a year. Dropbox's new limit for free accounts is six months.

How does Dropbox's 6-month rule compare with other cloud storage?

It's shorter than most. Google allows 2 years, Microsoft gives Outlook.com and OneDrive 1 year, Apple's iCloud terms say 1 year with 30 days' email notice, and pCloud says 12 months. MEGA is stricter, at over 3 months for free accounts after notice.
