USPS rule (Oct 19, 2026): senders billed for unpaid foreign duties on packages, even if disputed, 6% interest

If a foreign country says duties weren't paid on your package, USPS can now bill you for them, even if you dispute it or the package is lost, with 6% interest.

What changed

The U.S. Postal Service published an interim final rule in the Federal Register on October 19, 2026 (FR Doc. 2026-21310, "Customs Requirements and Payment Procedures for Certain Customs Charges") that took effect the same day. It rewrites the International Mail Manual so the person who mails a package abroad can be billed later for the destination country's duties, taxes and import fees. A new section 424, "Unpaid Destination Customs Charges," says that if a customs authority, postal operator or broker in the destination country "seeks to collect duties, taxes, or import fees from the mailer or the Postal Service" and they "have not been paid in full, the Postal Service (or its representative) will send an invoice to the mailer for such amounts."

The mailer "must pay the Postal Service ... the entire amount due in accordance with the invoice terms, even if the mailer disputes the assessment," and is not relieved of the charge "regardless of whether the item is delivered, lost, damaged, or returned." Late payment accrues "interest ... at a rate of 6 percent per annum," and USPS can recover its collection costs and may treat the unpaid amount as a postage revenue deficiency.

You can dispute only USPS accounting errors. Any challenge to the foreign charge itself "must be addressed directly to such party without involvement from the Postal Service," and USPS says it "is not responsible for the accuracy" of those charges. The rule also says that where a destination country requires a delivered duty paid (DDP) shipment, "the mailer must send the mailpiece on a delivered duty paid basis," and that duties and fees "are not included in the postage paid by the mailer."

USPS says the trigger is the European Union ending its de minimis exemption for most low-value goods on July 1, 2026. Under the new EU rules, some EU postal operators require prepaid duties, charge fees when customs data is wrong, or go after the sender when the recipient doesn't pay. USPS's own prepaid-duties page says EU countries now charge a flat, nonrefundable €3 duty on merchandise up to €150 and on gifts worth over €45 up to €150, and that USPS Delivered Duty Paid is built into retail counters and Click-N-Ship.

The rule text itself isn't limited to the EU: it applies wherever a destination party seeks unpaid charges. USPS is taking comments until November 18, 2026.

What it replaceschecked October 19, 2026

The International Mail Manual still posted on USPS's Postal Explorer site hasn't been updated yet. Its section 131.34 says "No provision is made for prepayment of customs duty on mail addressed for delivery in foreign countries," that "most countries will collect from the recipient," and that duties and fees "are not included in the postage paid by the mailer." It has no section 424 and doesn't mention invoicing the sender.

How private carriers compare

FedEx's duties-and-taxes page says that if the recipient refuses the package or refuses to pay, "the original shipper will be billed for duties and taxes." UPS tells shippers "if the receiver does not pay, UPS may recover the outstanding amounts from you." DHL Express's Terms and Conditions of Carriage say the shipper "shall pay or reimburse DHL for all Customs Duties and other charges ... if Consignee has failed to pay." So USPS is moving toward how commercial carriers already work.

Its version goes further in writing: pay "even if the mailer disputes the assessment," keep owing it if the item is lost or returned, 6% interest, and disputes limited to USPS accounting errors.

What you can do before you mail

Prepay with USPS Delivered Duty Paid. It's available only at the time of mailing, on Priority Mail Express International, Priority Mail International and First-Class Package International Service, through a retail counter, Click-N-Ship, USPS APIs or Global Shipping Software, and only to select countries. USPS's July 1, 2026 Postal Bulletin added 21 destinations, mostly EU countries and territories, including France, Italy, Spain and the Netherlands.

Describe each item accurately on the customs form, because USPS uses the descriptions to assign HS codes and charges the €3 duty "for each unique HS Code." The European Commission says the €3 applies per item type (one T-shirt plus one watch is €6), that the seller or importer is normally the one who pays, and that product identifiers become mandatory on November 1, 2026. Etsy's help center recommends DDP shipping to reduce "refused shipments, and unexpected charges at delivery." eBay sellers using eBay International Shipping send items to a U.S. hub instead, and eBay says import fees are collected from the buyer at checkout or on delivery.

How to comment

This is an interim final rule, so it's already in force, but USPS is taking comments until November 18, 2026. The Federal Register notice says to email PCFederalRegister@usps.gov with the subject line "Customs Requirements and Payment Procedures" and your name and address, or mail comments to the Manager of Product Classification, U.S. Postal Service, 475 L'Enfant Plaza SW, Room 4446, Washington, DC 20260-5015. Faxed comments aren't accepted, and on October 19 regulations.gov listed the document (USPS-2026-2511-0001) as not open for comment there.

Who it affects

Anyone who mails gifts or goods abroad with USPS, especially to EU countries ahead of the holidays; eBay, Etsy and other small sellers who ship internationally through USPS; and senders whose recipients refuse or fail to pay duties on delivery
